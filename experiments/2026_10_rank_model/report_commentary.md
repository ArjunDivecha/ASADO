## overview

**Data-integrity note, 7 October 2026.** A forensic audit of REER (`A Complete/T2 Factor Timing Fuzzy/Archive/REER_Audit_20261007/REPORT.md`) found that BIS rescales each country's REER so that its average in a common reference year — 2010 until January 2023, 2020 since — equals 100. Historical levels therefore embed each country's *future* real appreciation toward that year, and ranking countries by REER level sorts partly on it. With the data actually available at each decision date, REER's top-basket excess over 2014–2026 falls from 7.5% to 0.8% a year. This explains why REER came out as the single strongest factor in stage 2 (it is an artifact, not a signal), and it is why stages 4, 5 and 8 now show the **cleaned v3 panel**: no REER in any form, no OECD composites (normalised over the full history), and no cross-sectional z-score of any index level whose scale depends on a base year. The v1 numbers remain in the ablations section, pinned, for comparison. The wider lesson applies to every number on the page: all of this history is current-vintage data, and the random-month and walk-forward tests remove model-fitting look-ahead but not data-revision look-ahead.

**Where things stand: the default model.** The project's default since 7 October — the shared network retrained every year on only the trailing five years, thirty nets averaged, holding a name while the model still ranks it in the top 16 — beats the equal-weight average by **3.1% a year** out of sample across 260 months from 2005 to 2026, with a t-statistic of 2.8 and an information ratio of 0.59 (stage 11). Three independent runs of the whole walk-forward made 3.0%, 3.3% and 3.0%, so for the first time the headline does not depend on which run you look at. The edge is even across the decades — 2.7%, 3.3% and 3.1% a year — rather than front-loaded like the expanding-window version's, and the basket replaces 1.8 of its eight names a month. Two things temper it. The figure is uncertain by about ±1.1% a year (one standard error), so the honest range is roughly 1 to 5%. And the broad linear model on the same window is close: 1.95% under the same basket rule and 2.9% on the plain top-8 rule, so the net's margin under the default rule — 1.2 points, t 1.1 — makes it the better-behaved model here rather than a proven better one. The earlier headlines on this page (4.1%, 4.5%) came from single runs and are withdrawn (stage 10).

Everything else on the page is the road to that number: the factor map (stage 1), the univariate screen (2), the frozen panel (3), the floor that showed trees overfit and a linear model matches a single factor (4), the net that clears it (5), the ablations that showed it needs neither REER, nor GDELT, nor the coverage structure, nor global context, nor cross-country attention (6), the hill-climb that showed regularising it hurts while width and ensembling help (7), the walk-forward (8), the design holdout that tempers it (9), the rolling-window test with seed-draw replication that corrects it (10), and the default model that came out of all of it (11).

## stage1

Your instinct about the news factors was right, and they are not the only redundant family. The duplication sits in four places. The 46 GDELT news series (in each normalisation) collapse into roughly eight distinct things: two pairs are exact mirrors of each other, the `_raw` and `_z` variants track their parent at 0.98 or better, and the fast, slow, local and risk sentiment measures form one block. Twelve government yields, policy rates and money-market rates sit in a single block. Market cap, index weight, ETF assets, article count and the geopolitical-risk index all move together above 0.95, because big countries get more news and more index weight. And the price-level series — `PX_LAST`, the total-return index, the 120-day average, EPS levels — correlate above 0.9 with each other because a cross-sectional z-score of an index level in local currency is just units, not a signal.

The CS and TS versions of a factor are genuinely different views for almost everything: the median correlation between them is 0.37, and only 19 of the 151 factors with both exceed 0.8. Every one of those 19 is GDELT, whose series are standardised upstream so both normalisations land in the same place. That is why both versions are kept for the model, and why the de-duplication happens within each normalisation rather than across.

About thirty variables are junk for a 34-country model before you choose anything: 22 ECB exchange-rate series that each cover exactly one country, seven series that exist for a single month (the credit ratings, ETF creation fee and unit size, OFAC sanctions), and one Bloomberg debt ratio with one country and 38 rows. They show as the grey stripes in the heatmap.

## stage2

**Read REER's row in light of the 7 October audit:** its t-statistic of 3.4 and 5.0% a year basket are what a retrospectively rebased level series looks like, not evidence of a tradable factor. The same caution applies to the cross-sectional form of any index level with a base-year or full-history scale (CPI index, OECD composite indicators).

The univariate signal is thin. Of the 262 factors with enough history to score, 23 clear a t-statistic of 1.96 — against about 13 you would expect by chance from 262 tries — and only one survives a false-discovery correction. The best honest top-8 baskets make four to five percent a year over the equal-weight average with hit rates around 0.6, and the median factor makes under one percent.

The strongest names are familiar. REER from T2 has a t-statistic of 3.4 and its top-8 basket makes 5.0% a year at a 0.61 hit rate. Advance-decline is 3.2 on 229 months. One-month reversal in time-series form is 3.1, and Best ROE in time-series form is 2.7. Trailing PE and earnings yield each make about 4% a year in the basket at 0.57 hit rates, though their t-statistics are only 1.4 and 1.8. The best top-8 number anywhere is GDELT tone dispersion at 5.4% a year with a 0.62 hit rate — but that is 133 months since 2015, and one of a dozen near-identical news series, so it is one hit tested many ways. Time-series variants screen a bit better than cross-sectional ones overall, 14 significant versus 9.

Two things surprised me, and both matter for the model build. First, **T2's REER is the BIS REER shifted one month**: the T2 value at any date equals the BIS value from the month before, exactly, across all 10,494 country-months. That is why T2 REER at lag zero and BIS REER at lag one give the same answer while BIS REER at lag zero shows nothing. T2 bakes the publication lag into the stored series, and I would assume its other macro columns do the same. Second, **lag choice dominates for the slow sources**: across the 64 lagged-source factors the mean |t| is 0.9 at the harness lag and 1.9 at zero lag, with 18 significant versus 7. Most of that zero-lag "signal" is information that was not available in time, which is why the modelling panel applies the lags.

One artifact to ignore: `MS_Index_Weight_CS` shows a t of −3.1 but rests on 25 months at a 12-month lag. And 79 factors are unscored because they cover fewer than 20 countries or 24 months. This is in-sample over the full history by design — it ranks the factors, it does not validate them.

## stage3

The set is 256 factors: the 262 the screen could score, minus the six price-level and market-cap series. By source that is 98 from T2, 92 from GDELT, 31 from Bloomberg and 35 from the IMF, FRED, BIS, OECD and the uncertainty indices; 130 cross-sectional and 126 time-series; 190 used as stored and 64 lagged one month. Two of the 256 — `MS_Index_Weight` in both forms — are the 25-month, 12-month-lag artifacts from the screen; they are in by your rule but cover 7% of rows and will contribute nothing.

The coverage chart is the thing to keep in mind when reading every later result. Before 2015 a typical row has about 125 of the 256 factors; after 2016 it has about 232, because the whole GDELT block starts in 2015. The models are not allowed to drop the early rows — that would throw away half the history — so each handles the missingness its own way: the trees natively, the linear model by treating a missing z-score as average and carrying a per-source "how much of this block is present" column.

Still in the set, flagged rather than removed: the total-return index, the 120-day average, and the BEST and trailing EPS *levels*, which are scale rather than signal in cross-sectional form. They were not on your drop list, so they stay — your call.

The lag application was verified exactly: every lagged column at month D equals the raw value at D−1, and every unlagged column equals the raw value at D. The excess-return target averages to zero within every month by construction.

## stage4

On the cleaned panel the floor is a little lower and the shape is the same. Ridge over the 238 factors makes 4.7% a year over the equal-weight average on evaluation months of the thirty random splits, hit rate 0.60, rank correlation 0.07, long-minus-short spread 9.5%. That is 0.6 points below its v1 figure (t = −1.6) — the no-REER ablation alone had cost it nothing, so the missing half-point came from the other series the audit rule removed (the OECD composites and the cross-sectional index levels), and the walk-forward in stage 8 says that half-point was look-ahead rather than information. The ridge penalty chosen inside the training months moved up a decade, to mostly 10 to 100, which is what you expect once the single strongest (and cleanest-looking) input is gone.

The trees are below ridge by 2.7 to 2.9 points a year with t-statistics of −4 to −5 — the regression variant 2.0%, the top-8 classifier 1.7%, lambdarank 1.9% — while fitting their training months at 12 to 23% a year. On the contiguous blocks ridge makes 1.9% and the tree regression goes negative. The shuffled-label controls sit at 0.8% and −0.7%, zero within the ±2.7% single-split noise. The single-factor reference is gone from this stage on purpose: REER was the reference, and the audit retired it.

Nothing about the floor's verdict changed: on ten thousand rows of noisy labels, a regularised linear model is as good as it gets without a neural network, trees memorise, and the number to beat is about 4.7% on random splits and 2% on blocks.

## stage5

The net clears the clean floor by one and a half points a year. As ten-seed ensembles of the 256/128 network on the thirty random splits, the three objectives make 6.2% (predict-then-select), 6.1% (soft top-8) and 6.0% a year (warm-start) over the equal-weight average, against ridge's 4.7% on the same months; the paired differences are +1.5, +1.4 and +1.3 points with t-statistics of 2.7, 2.3 and 2.1, and the predict-then-select net wins 19 of 30 splits. Hit rates are 0.60 to 0.61, rank correlation 0.09, and the long-minus-short spread 11.7 to 12.8% a year against ridge's 9.5. On the contiguous blocks the predict-then-select net makes 5.1% against ridge's 1.9%. The shuffled-label control sits at 0.7%.

Against the same architecture on the v1 panel (the "new base" in the ablations) the clean net is 0.9 points lower, t = −3.1, so the contaminated series were worth about a point to the net on random splits — less than they were worth to the trees, and, as stage 8 shows, nothing to the net out of sample.

The objectives tie, as before; the soft objective still buys a wider long-short spread rather than a better basket. Ensembling still does a third of the work: single nets make 4.3 to 5.5% a year, the ten-seed averages 6.0 to 6.2. Training fit is 34% a year for the predict-then-select net and 43 to 45% for the soft objectives, which the hill-climb showed is not the problem it looks like. The 2000–2009 pattern persists on clean inputs: the net's margin over ridge is 3.5 points a year in that decade with a t-statistic of 2.5, 1.3 points in the 2010s (t = 1.2), and nothing since 2020. The capacity check fitted 24 months perfectly.

## ablation_noreer

REER is not load-bearing. With all four REER columns removed — the T2 and BIS versions in both normalisations — the broad linear model makes 5.2% a year on evaluation months against 5.3% with them, a paired difference of −0.04 with a t-statistic of −0.1; it was worse on 17 of 30 splits and better on 13, which is a coin toss. Its era profile is unchanged too (7.7% in the 2000s without REER against 6.8% with). The ridge result was never REER wearing 256 hats; the information REER carries is spread across the nominal-currency, inflation and rate factors that remain, and a linear combination finds it there just as easily.

The trees are the model that cared. LightGBM regression falls from 3.8% to 1.9% a year, worse on 20 of 30 splits with a t-statistic of −3.6, and `BIS_REER_CS` had been its single most-used feature. Without it the trees lean on realised volatility, short-term reversal and advance-decline instead, and transfer less.

The nets lose a little and keep their shape. The three objectives make 5.8%, 5.9% and 6.1% a year without REER against 6.3 to 6.5% with it — about half a point, worse on 17 to 20 splits of 30, t-statistics between −1.3 and −1.5, so not something to lean on in either direction. On the contiguous blocks the predict-then-select net is unchanged or slightly better (5.2% against 4.7%). The 2000–2009 edge is still there without REER (10.1% against 10.6% a year), so whatever the net found in that decade is not REER either. What does change is the net's margin over the broad linear model: it narrows from about 1.1–1.3 points to 0.5–0.85 points, with t-statistics between 1.0 and 1.5 and wins on 18 to 21 of 30 splits. The shuffled-label control sits at 0.5%, zero within noise.

What to take from it: the broad model's number is robust to dropping its single strongest input, which is the property you wanted. The net's advantage over the broad model is real but small and partly rode on REER; a half-to-one-point edge that needs a 30-split paired test to see is the honest size of it before the hill-climb.

## stage7

*This stage was run on the v1 panel (with REER); its conclusions — regularisation hurts, weight decay is irrelevant, width helps weakly, ensembling saturates near ten seeds — are about the network, not the inputs, and were carried to the v3 runs unchanged.*

The sweep found nothing that beats the base configuration, and it found the opposite of what I expected. Every attempt to regularise the network harder makes it worse on the evaluation months: dropout 0.30 costs 1.2 points a year (t = −3.4, wins only 10 of 30 splits), dropout 0.50 costs 2.0 (t = −4.3), the two "heavy" combinations cost 1.3 and 1.4 (t ≈ −3), and a slower learning rate costs 2.0 (t = −4.9, wins 6 of 30). Those nets also fit their training months far less — 16 to 24% a year against the base's 30% — so the memorisation that looked like the obvious lever was not what was hurting. Early stopping after thirty-odd epochs and the five-seed average were already doing the regularising; constraining the network on top of that just removed signal.

Weight decay is irrelevant: 0.1 and 1.0 land within a tenth of a point of the base. Width matters a little and in the direction of bigger: halving to 32/16 costs half a point (t = −1.1), doubling to 128/64 changes nothing, and quadrupling to 256/128 adds a third of a point on random splits (6.7% against 6.3%, t = 1.1, wins 14 of 30 — a tie) but is the best config on the contiguous blocks by a wide margin, 6.6% against the base's 4.7%. It is consistent with the regularisation finding: this problem wants more capacity, not less.

The seed curve is the clean result. A single net averages 4.6% a year; two averaged make 5.3%, three 5.5%, five 6.3%, eight 6.7%, ten 6.8%, and fifteen or twenty stay at 6.6 to 6.7%. Averaging saturates around eight to ten nets and adds a little over two points over a single one — more than any architectural choice. The base configuration rerun inside the sweep reproduced the stage-5 numbers to four decimal places on every split, so the training is deterministic and the comparisons are exact.

## ablation_base256

The hill-climb's recommendation held up when applied: the wider net with ten seeds makes 7.1% a year on evaluation months on the predict-then-select objective, against 6.3% for the stage-5 net, a paired gain of 0.75 points with a t-statistic of 2.6, better on 21 of 30 splits. The soft and warm-start objectives gain less (0.5 and 0.3 points, inside the noise) and the three objectives still tie with one another. On the contiguous blocks the predict-then-select net rises from 4.7% to 6.1% and the warm-start net from 4.3% to 6.6%. Against the broad linear model on the same splits the new base is 1.8 points a year ahead with a t-statistic of 3.2, better on 23 of 30 — the clearest margin any model has shown. Its training-month fit also rose, from 30% to 35% a year, which after the hill-climb is no longer something to worry about. This run is on the v1 panel, with REER still in; the cleaned v3 rerun is the number to carry forward.

## ablation_nogdelt

The net does not need the news block. With all 92 GDELT factors removed, the predict-then-select net makes 6.6% a year against 7.1%, a half-point drop with a t-statistic of −1.4 that the other two objectives do not share (they are up 0.2); ridge is unchanged at 5.4%. On the blocks the predict-then-select net falls from 6.1% to 4.0%, which with five blocks is suggestive rather than a finding. The point of this ablation was the decade question: GDELT only exists from 2015, so it cannot be the source of the 2000–2009 edge directly, but its absence before 2015 shapes the coverage pattern a net could read. Without GDELT the early-decade margin over ridge is still 3.3 points a year with a t-statistic of 2.3. Not GDELT.

## ablation_nopresence

Removing the fifteen per-source presence columns — the net's explicit view of which blocks of data exist in a given row — changes nothing that survives a t-test: the three objectives move by −0.4, +0.2 and +0.3 points, ridge by 0.02. The 2000–2009 margin over ridge is 3.5 points a year with a t-statistic of 2.7, the same as with them. So the early-decade edge is not the net reading the coverage structure of the early rows either. Between this and the GDELT run, what the net found in 2000–2009 is in the factor values of that era. Whether it is economics or a data-vintage artifact of the kind the REER audit found is exactly what the cleaned v3 rerun and the walk-forward are for.

## ablation_global

Global context added nothing. With VIX, the two- and ten-year Treasury yields and their spread, the broad dollar and the three global geopolitical-risk indices appended as inputs — all as time-series z-scores, lagged a month — the three nets move by −0.3, −0.2 and 0.0 points a year, ridge by −0.06, the trees by +0.03, every one inside the noise. On the contiguous blocks the predict-then-select net drops from 6.1% to 4.9%, which is in the direction of "more inputs to memorise" but not a finding on five blocks. The by-era pattern is unchanged. I expected the opposite: a global variable fingerprints the month, and in a random-month split that should have let the net recognise held-out months from their neighbours and inflated the score. It did not, which says the net was already extracting whatever era information the panel carries from the country factors themselves, and that the interactions these series could enable — cheap works when risk appetite is high — are not there to find in 34 countries and 320 months. The shuffled-label control sits at 1.2%, zero within noise. Not a lever.

## ablation_attn

Letting countries see each other did not help. The cross-country attention model — each month's 34 countries as a set of tokens, two transformer layers of 64 dimensions, four heads, five seeds — ties the shared MLP on the soft top-8 objective (6.6% a year against 6.8%, a paired difference of −0.2 with t = −0.6) and beats ridge by 1.3 points with a t-statistic of 2.2. On the predict-then-select objective it failed outright: 2.7% a year, 4.4 points below the MLP with a t-statistic of −6.9, and below ridge. Its training-month fit on that objective is only 8% a year against 35% for the MLP, so this is an optimisation failure rather than overfitting — the layer-normalised output of a transformer does not easily produce the raw return scale a squared-error loss demands, while the soft objective only needs the ordering and trained normally (fit 49%). It could be fixed with an output scale or a longer schedule, but the soft-objective result already answers the architectural question: at this data size, a country's score does not gain from the other 33 countries' inputs beyond what the cross-sectional z-scores already encode. The attention net also costs about seven times the MLP's compute per net. The shuffled-label control sits at 0.5%. Not a lever.

## stage8



**Correction (stage 10).** Every figure in this section comes from one seed draw of the walk-forward. Rerunning the identical design with two further independent draws gave 1.5% and 2.6% a year with the default basket rule, against 4.5% here, and pooling all thirty nets gives 2.95% (t 2.3). The 4.5% was the high draw; read the numbers below as one sample from a range, and use stage 10's pooled figure as the estimate.

**The headline rule (project default since 7 October 2026).** The basket is formed from the walk-forward's out-of-sample scores by holding a name while the model still ranks it in the top 16 of the universe and replacing only names that fall below — "still in the top half". On that rule the predict-then-select net makes **4.5% a year** over the equal-weight average across the 260 out-of-sample months, t-statistic 3.4, hit rate 0.55, information ratio 0.72, replacing 1.8 names a month (268% one-way turnover a year); ridge makes 2.25% (t 1.9); the net's margin over ridge is 2.25 points with a t-statistic of 1.8, and the soft-objective net, which the plain rule had left at 2.4%, makes 4.3% (t 3.3). The plain rule is reported alongside in every table and is the basis of the paragraphs that follow, which were written on it; nothing in them changes in substance, and the hysteresis paragraph at the end gives the sweep the default was chosen from.

This is the number the whole project was built to produce, and it is the net's. Trained each year only on the years before it — starting with five years of history and expanding, 22 folds, 260 months from February 2005 to September 2026 — the predict-then-select net beats the equal-weight average by **4.1% a year** in its top-8 basket, with a t-statistic of 3.0, a hit rate of 0.56, and a positive year in fifteen of twenty-two. It caught 2.3 of the true top eight on average and its long-minus-short spread was 6.5% a year. The whole-period figure is uncertain by about ±1.3% a year (one standard error), so the honest range is roughly 1.5 to 6.7%.

The cleaning is what makes this the result rather than a caveat. On the v1 panel the net made 4.0% a year out of sample; on the clean panel, 4.1% — paired month by month the difference is 0.07 points with a t-statistic of 0.1. The net never needed REER or the other rebased levels. The broad linear model did: ridge made 4.6% a year out of sample with them and 2.1% without, a paired loss of 2.4 points with a t-statistic of −2.6, and its clean figure has a t-statistic of only 1.7. That is the REER audit's lesson stated for the broad model — a walk-forward removes model look-ahead, not data look-ahead, and more than half of what looked like forecastable edge in a linear combination of 256 factors was a future base year leaking through monotone level series. A linear model loves a monotone series with look-ahead in it; the net, it turns out, was not leaning on those.

So the net's margin over the clean linear model out of sample is 2.0 points a year, with a t-statistic of 1.5 and the net ahead in 51% of months — suggestive, and reversed from the v1 walk-forward where the two tied, but not established. By era the net makes 5.8% a year in 2005–2009 (t = 1.9, on folds trained with five to nine years of history), 3.7% in 2010–2019 (t = 2.3) and 3.4% since 2020 (t = 1.2); ridge makes 1.5, 1.5 and 3.5. The soft-objective net is weaker through time, 2.4% with a t-statistic of 2.0 — the objective that ties on random splits does not tie out of sample, and predict-then-select is the one to carry.

What the year-by-year table should temper. The swings are large: the net lost 9% in 2006, 6.6% in 2024 and made 17% in 2009, 14% in 2012 and 18.5% in 2022; ridge's best year is 8.9% and its worst −6.4%. The early folds are the noisiest. And one caveat applies to the design rather than the weights: the architecture, width, seed count, objective and stopping rule were all chosen on random splits of the same 320 months, so the walk-forward is out of sample for what the net learned each year but not for how it was built. A clean version of this test would choose the design on the first half of the history and walk forward only on the second; that is cheap and is the first thing to run next.

What surprised me: that cleaning the inputs cost the linear model half its out-of-sample edge and the net none of it. I had expected the opposite ordering — a flexible model to be the one exploiting look-ahead — and the result says the leakage was in the direction a linear model follows naturally.

**Hysteresis (added after the turnover question).** The plain rule re-picks the top eight from scratch every month and replaces 3.8 names on average — about half the book, 570% a year one way. Holding a name until the model drops it out of the top 16 replaces 1.8 names a month, 268% a year, and the out-of-sample excess does not pay for it: 4.5% a year against 4.1%, t-statistic 3.4 against 3.0, information ratio 0.72 against 0.65, longest underwater stretch 38 months against 49, and positive in all three eras (6.2%, 3.4%, 4.9%). The curve is flat between M = 12 and M = 16 and only starts to cost at M = 20 (3.3%), so the choice is not delicate; I would fix M = 16 a priori — "hold while still in the top half" — rather than tune it, because the sweep was read on the out-of-sample months. Ridge's turnover falls the same way (519% to 191%) with its excess flat at about 2.2%, and the soft-objective net, whose scores are noisier at the top, gains the most from a buffer (2.4% to 4.3% at M = 16). For scale, every 10bp of one-way cost per trade would take about 0.3% a year off the buffered basket against 0.6% off the plain one.

## stage9

The design holdout half-confirms and half-undoes the headline. The half it confirms: choosing every design element on the first half alone picks the same network — 256/128 hidden units, dropout 0.15, weight decay 0.01, learning rate 1e-3 — and the first-half seed curve saturates at eight to ten nets just as the full-history one did. The architecture was not a product of seeing the later months. The half it undoes: walked blind through the second half — June 2013 to September 2026, 160 months nothing was tuned on — that network makes 2.0% a year over the equal-weight average on the plain rule, with a t-statistic of 1.3, and the broad linear model makes 3.2% (t 2.1). The net does not beat it: the paired difference is −1.2 points with a t-statistic of −0.7.

The basket buffer is the one design choice the first half made differently. Its sweep peaks at M = 10 (4.2% a year over 2005–2013), not 16, and that choice does worse in the second half — 1.4% a year — than either the plain rule or M = 16 (2.1%). A hundred months is too few to pick a buffer width from. What does carry over is the turnover result: in the second half, holding while ranked in the top 16 cuts one-way turnover from 638% to 317% a year while the excess moves from 2.0% to 2.1%. The buffer is free; its exact width is not something this data can choose.

The most important number in this stage is a correlation. The stage-8 walk-forward also scored these 160 months, with the same network and the same data, differing only in that its yearly refits fall in February rather than June and draw different seeds. Over the same months it made 2.7% a year on the plain rule and 3.9% with the buffer, against 2.0% and 2.1% here — and the month-by-month excess of the two nets' baskets correlates at only 0.21. For ridge, which has no seeds and only moves with the training window, the correlation is 0.63 to 0.71. So even a ten-seed ensemble of this net picks substantially different baskets from one innocuous implementation choice to the next, and the variation between runs is about as large as the edge. The t-statistics on this page measure month-to-month noise for a fixed model; they do not include this run-to-run noise, so they overstate how precisely the net's edge is known.

Splitting the stage-8 walk at the same point shows the shape behind the headline: the net made 6.4% a year in 2005–2013 (t 2.9) and 2.7% since (t 1.6); ridge made 2.3% and 2.1%. Whatever the net found that the linear model did not is concentrated in the first decade — consistent with every random-split result since stage 5.

What this changes: the 4.5% stands as a description of 2005–2026, but the claim that the net beats the broad linear model does not survive a design held out from the months it is scored on, and the net's edge is less stable than its t-statistic implies. If this goes further, the next step is about stability rather than architecture: refit every month instead of every year, which removes the calendar choice entirely, average many more seeds, and repeat this holdout. If the second-half net still sits near 2% and ties ridge, the right production model is the simpler one.

What surprised me: that the month in which the model is refit mattered about as much as the choice between a neural network and a linear model.

## stage10

Training only on the trailing five years makes no difference that survives a test. For the net, pooled across thirty nets, the rolling window makes 2.7% a year on the plain rule and 3.7% with the buffer, against 2.6% and 3.0% for the expanding window; the paired differences are +0.1 and +0.7 points with t-statistics of 0.1 and 0.6. Pairing the draws one by one instead of pooling them gives +0.2 and −0.7 — the same comparison changes sign depending on how the runs are combined, which is what a difference made of noise looks like. For ridge, which is deterministic, the rolling window makes 2.9% on the plain rule and 1.95% with the buffer, against 2.1% and 2.25% — differences of +0.75 and −0.3, t-statistics of 0.6 and −0.2, in opposite directions. Recent history does not forecast better than all history here, and it does not forecast worse; the five-year window simply costs nothing. If anything favours it, it is the second half, where the pooled rolling net with the buffer makes 3.7% a year (t 2.3) against 3.0% for the expanding one — but that is one cell of eight, picked after seeing them, and not something to adopt on this evidence.

The more important result came from running everything three times. The identical expanding-window design, retrained with three independent seed draws (each draw changes the ten nets' seeds and the early-stopping slice), made 4.5%, 1.5% and 2.6% a year with the buffer, and 4.1%, 2.0% and 1.3% on the plain rule. The stage-8 headline was the top draw. Each draw is itself a ten-net ensemble, and the three draws' baskets still correlate only 0.6 month to month (0.52 to 0.55 for the rolling window). Pooling the three draws into one thirty-net ensemble per fold raises the expected result and steadies it: 2.95% (t 2.3) with the buffer and 2.6% (t 2.0) on the plain rule for the expanding window — so roughly 3% a year is the honest figure for this net over 2005–2026.

Against ridge, the pooled net is ahead by 0.5 to 0.7 points a year with the expanding window (t 0.4 to 0.6) and by −0.2 to +1.7 with the rolling one (t up to 1.4). None of it is significant. The hysteresis buffer, by contrast, behaved the same in every draw: about 1.8 names replaced a month instead of 3.7, with the excess unchanged or higher in five of the six net draws.

What this changes: the project's headline is about 3% a year out of sample, not 4.5%, and the network is not distinguishable from the linear model. Any further work with the net needs a large pooled ensemble — thirty nets at least, with varied early-stopping slices — before a single number is worth reading, and every comparison should be reported across several independent draws, as here.

What surprised me: how much a ten-net ensemble still depends on which ten nets. I had treated ten seeds as enough because the random-split seed curve flattened there; the walk-forward, where each fold trains on less data, is far more seed-sensitive than the random splits suggested.

## stage11

The default model makes 3.1% a year over the equal-weight average out of sample, and it is the first number on this page that survives being rerun. Three independent runs of the entire walk-forward — each retraining thirty nets every year on the trailing five years, with its own seeds and early-stopping slices — made 3.05%, 3.28% and 3.02% a year. Holding the three runs' baskets in equal thirds gives 3.1% with a t-statistic of 2.8, an information ratio of 0.59 and a hit rate of 0.56; pooling all ninety nets into one ensemble gives 3.2%. Over 260 months that figure is uncertain by about ±1.1% a year.

Two things make it stable, and one of them I did not expect. Thirty nets instead of ten is the obvious one: in stage 10 the ten-net rolling runs with the buffer made 2.7%, 2.0% and 1.9%; at thirty they agree, and going to ninety adds nothing. The other is the buffer. The same thirty-net runs on the plain re-pick-the-top-eight rule made 1.6%, 3.1% and 1.9% — the spread is back. Runs disagree mostly about the countries ranked eighth to twelfth, and a rule that holds a name until it falls out of the top sixteen ignores most of that disagreement. The runs' monthly baskets still correlate only 0.59, but over a year the differences wash out.

The shape is even. By decade the default model made 2.7% a year in 2005–2009 (t 1.0), 3.3% in the 2010s (t 2.3) and 3.1% since 2020 (t 1.5) — the expanding-window version made most of its money before 2013, and the rolling window removes that front-loading, which is consistent with your reason for preferring it. Fifteen of twenty-two years were positive. The worst was 2006 (−7.8%) and the best 2009 (+13.8%); 2024 and 2025 were both negative (−4.9% and −3.1%) before +7.4% so far in 2026. The largest relative drawdown was 12.6%, from December 2005 to May 2008, recovered by July 2009; the longest stretch below a previous high was 42 months. The excess has a volatility of 5.2% a year. In absolute terms the basket compounded at 11.2% a year against 8.3% for the equal-weight average.

Against ridge on the same rolling window: under the default rule ridge makes 1.95% (t 1.6) and is still in a relative drawdown that began in August 2021, 61 months below its high; on the plain rule it makes 2.9% (t 2.2). The buffer suits the net and not ridge here. Under the default rule the net is 1.2 points ahead (t 1.1); under the plain rule it is 0.7 behind (t −0.6). Each model's better rule lands near 3%. The net is not shown to be the better forecaster; it is the more stable and better-behaved of the two under the rule we would trade with — shallower drawdowns, not under water now, the same answer every run.

The soft top-8 objective was run alongside and is less stable under the default — 3.9%, 2.5% and 2.3% across the three runs — so predict-then-select stays the default objective.

What this does not settle. The inputs are current-vintage, so revision look-ahead in the slow macro series is still unmeasured. The design was chosen with the whole history in view; the check consistent with the rolling view is to re-choose the design every year on the trailing five years only, which I expect to change little because the first-half choice in stage 9 was the same network. And all of it is gross of costs: at 1.8 names a month (about 264% one-way a year), every 10 basis points of one-way cost per trade would take roughly 0.26% a year off the 3.1%.

What surprised me: that the buffer, which I added to cut turnover, turned out to be what makes the net's runs agree with each other.

## next

The default is fixed: rolling five years, thirty nets, predict-then-select, hold while ranked in the top 16, cleaned 238-factor panel, about 3.1% a year out of sample with a range across runs of 3.0 to 3.3%. What would move it from a research result toward a decision:

- **Forward paper-trading.** Freeze the default, score each month as it arrives, record the basket and its excess. Nothing on this page substitutes for that.
- **Vintage-archived slow inputs** and the T2 macro publication lag (`docs/USER_FIX_LIST.md`) — the largest remaining unmeasured source of look-ahead.
- **Re-choose the design every year on the trailing five years** (a nested rolling walk-forward), if you want the selection question closed in a way that respects "the world changes"; about 20 minutes of compute.
- **Implementation costs** at the measured 1.8 names a month, at implementation time rather than as a research penalty.
