## overview

**Data-integrity note, 7 October 2026.** A forensic audit of REER (`A Complete/T2 Factor Timing Fuzzy/Archive/REER_Audit_20261007/REPORT.md`) found that BIS rescales each country's REER so that its average in a common reference year — 2010 until January 2023, 2020 since — equals 100. Historical levels therefore embed each country's *future* real appreciation toward that year, and ranking countries by REER level sorts partly on it. With the data actually available at each decision date, REER's top-basket excess over 2014–2026 falls from 7.5% to 0.8% a year. This explains why REER came out as the single strongest factor in stage 2 (it is an artifact, not a signal), it means the "reference" line in stages 4 and 5 is not a legitimate yardstick, and it is the reason the project moves to a cleaned factor set (v3: no REER in any form, and no cross-sectional z-score of any index level whose scale depends on a base year or on full-history normalisation). The stage-6 ablation already showed the broad linear model does not depend on REER — 5.2% a year without it against 5.3% with — so the headline survives. The wider lesson is written on every later stage: all of this history is current-vintage data, and the random-month and walk-forward tests remove model-fitting look-ahead but not data-revision look-ahead.

Stages 1 to 5 are complete: duplicates mapped, every factor screened alone, your 256-factor set frozen into one lag-correct modelling table, a linear-and-trees floor established, and the first neural network trained — all on the same thirty random partitions of the months and five contiguous blocks, so every comparison is paired.

**Where things stand.** The broad linear model (ridge over all 256 factors) makes 5.3% a year over the equal-weight average in its top-8 basket on months it has not seen, and that is the bar — the single-factor reference ties it but is not something to build on. The boosted trees fall short of the bar and memorise. The first neural network clears it: 6.3 to 6.5% a year depending on the training objective, about one point over ridge, winning 20 to 22 of the 30 splits with a paired t-statistic of 2.1 to 2.3, and holding up at least as well as ridge on contiguous blocks. The shuffled-label control sits at zero. The caveat that matters: **the net's edge over ridge comes entirely from 2000–2009**; since 2010 the two are tied at roughly 3.5 to 5% a year. The three ways of training the net — predict returns then pick eight, train on the soft top-8 objective directly, or one then the other — tie on the top-8 basket. Removing REER entirely (all four variants) changes nothing for the broad linear model — 5.2% with or without — and costs the net about half a point, so the result is not one factor in disguise. The hill-climb then answered the obvious follow-up the wrong way round: regularising the net makes it worse — every stronger dropout, every heavier combination and a slower learning rate all lose to the base config by one to two points with t-statistics of 3 to 5 — while weight decay does nothing and a wider net is weakly better. Averaging more seeds is the one lever that pays, and it saturates at eight to ten. The ceiling for this architecture on these inputs is about 6.5 to 6.8% a year on random splits, one to one-and-a-half points over the broad linear model.

## stage1

Your instinct about the news factors was right, and they are not the only redundant family. The duplication sits in four places. The 46 GDELT news series (in each normalisation) collapse into roughly eight distinct things: two pairs are exact mirrors of each other, the `_raw` and `_z` variants track their parent at 0.98 or better, and the fast, slow, local and risk sentiment measures form one block. Twelve government yields, policy rates and money-market rates sit in a single block. Market cap, index weight, ETF assets, article count and the geopolitical-risk index all move together above 0.95, because big countries get more news and more index weight. And the price-level series — `PX_LAST`, the total-return index, the 120-day average, EPS levels — correlate above 0.9 with each other because a cross-sectional z-score of an index level in local currency is just units, not a signal.

The CS and TS versions of a factor are genuinely different views for almost everything: the median correlation between them is 0.37, and only 19 of the 151 factors with both exceed 0.8. Every one of those 19 is GDELT, whose series are standardised upstream so both normalisations land in the same place. That is why both versions are kept for the model, and why the de-duplication happens within each normalisation rather than across.

About thirty variables are junk for a 34-country model before you choose anything: 22 ECB exchange-rate series that each cover exactly one country, seven series that exist for a single month (the credit ratings, ETF creation fee and unit size, OFAC sanctions), and one Bloomberg debt ratio with one country and 38 rows. They show as the grey stripes in the heatmap.

## stage2

**Read REER's row in light of the 7 October audit:** its t-statistic of 3.4 and 5.0% a year basket are what a retrospectively rebased level series looks like, not evidence of a tradable factor. The same caution applies to the cross-sectional form of any index level with a base-year or full-history scale (CPI index, OECD composite indicators).

The univariate signal is thin. Of the 262 factors with enough history to score, 23 clear a t-statistic of 1.96 — against about 13 you would expect by chance from 262 tries — and only one survives a false-discovery correction. The best honest top-8 baskets make four to five percent a year over the equal-weight average with hit rates around 0.6, and the median factor makes under one percent.

The strongest names are familiar. REER from T2 has a t-statistic of 3.4 and its top-8 basket makes 5.0% a year at a 0.61 hit rate. Advance-decline is 3.2 on 229 months. One-month reversal in time-series form is 3.1, and Best ROE in time-series form is 2.7. Trailing PE and earnings yield each make about 4% a year in the basket at 0.57 hit rates, though their t-statistics are only 1.4 and 1.8. The best top-8 number anywhere is GDELT tone dispersion at 5.4% a year with a 0.62 hit rate — but that is 133 months since 2015, and one of a dozen near-identical news series, so it is one hit tested many ways. Time-series variants screen a bit better than cross-sectional ones overall, 14 significant versus 9.

Two things surprised me, and both matter for the model build. First, **T2's REER is the BIS REER shifted one month**: the T2 value at any date equals the BIS value from the month before, exactly, across all 10,494 country-months. That is why T2 REER at lag zero and BIS REER at lag one give the same answer while BIS REER at lag zero shows nothing. T2 bakes the publication lag into the stored series, and I would assume its other macro columns do the same. Second, **lag choice dominates for the slow sources**: across the 64 lagged-source factors the mean |t| is 0.9 at the harness lag and 1.9 at zero lag, with 18 significant versus 7. Most of that zero-lag "signal" is information that was not available in time, which is why the modelling panel applies the lags.

One artifact to ignore: `MS_Index_Weight_CS` shows a t of −3.1 but rests on 25 months at a 12-month lag. And 79 factors are unscored because they cover fewer than 20 countries or 24 months. This is in-sample over the full history by design — it ranks the factors, it does not validate them.

## stage3

The set is 256 factors: the 262 the screen could score, minus the six price-level and market-cap series. By source that is 98 from T2, 92 from GDELT, 31 from Bloomberg and 35 from the IMF, FRED, BIS, OECD and the uncertainty indices; 130 cross-sectional and 126 time-series; 190 used as stored and 64 lagged one month. Two of the 256 — `MS_Index_Weight` in both forms — are the 25-month, 12-month-lag artifacts from the screen; they are in by your rule but cover 7% of rows and will contribute nothing.

The coverage chart is the thing to keep in mind when reading every later result. Before 2015 a typical row has about 125 of the 256 factors; after 2016 it has about 232, because the whole GDELT block starts in 2015. The models are not allowed to drop the early rows — that would throw away half the history — so each handles the missingness its own way: the trees natively, the linear model by treating a missing z-score as average and carrying a per-source "how much of this block is present" column.

Still in the set, flagged rather than removed: the total-return index, the 120-day average, and the BEST and trailing EPS *levels*, which are scale rather than signal in cross-sectional form. They were not on your drop list, so they stay — your call.

The lag application was verified exactly: every lagged column at month D equals the raw value at D−1, and every unlagged column equals the raw value at D. The excess-return target averages to zero within every month by construction.

## stage4

The result is a tie between a 256-factor linear model and one factor. On the thirty random splits, ridge's top-8 basket makes 5.3% a year over the equal-weight average on evaluation months and REER alone makes 5.2%; the paired difference is +0.1% with a t-statistic of 0.2. Ridge wins six months in ten, REER wins six in ten. Pooling every evaluation month across the splits, ridge's edge has a t-statistic of 4.9, so it is real — it is just not bigger than what one factor already gives you.

The trees are worse, and the pattern is textbook overfitting. The LightGBM regression makes 3.8% a year on evaluation months, the top-8 classifier 2.8%, lambdarank 1.9%; all three are below the single factor by a margin that holds across splits (paired t-statistics of −3.4 to −4.9). On their training months the same models make 22–26% a year. The early-stopping record says the same thing from the other side: the trees typically stop after about a hundred rounds and on some splits after one, meaning the inner validation slice saw nothing worth learning past the first few splits. Ten thousand rows of noisy labels is not enough for trees to find interactions that transfer.

The controls behaved. Ridge and the tree regression refit on scrambled labels made 0.4% and 0.5% a year on evaluation months — inside the ±2.9% noise of a single split and, pooled over all months, statistically zero. The contiguous-block check is the more interesting diagnostic: held out in 64-month blocks, ridge drops from 5.3% to 3.2% a year while the reference factor, which involves no fitting, stays at 5.0%. So about two points of the random-split score is neighbouring months leaking across the split, and roughly 3% a year is the better estimate of what the linear model transfers through time. The tree regression falls to 0.3% on blocks — essentially nothing.

Three things worth keeping from this. First, what ridge adds is not in the top eight but in the bottom: its rank correlation with next-month return is 0.09 against REER's 0.05, and its long-minus-short spread is 10.9% a year against 6.7%, because the linear combination is much better at identifying the countries to avoid. If the objective were ever long-short, the model story would be different. Second, ridge's edge is steadier than REER's: by five-year era the ridge basket made between 3.4% and 8.6% a year in every period, while REER's ran from 9.5% in 2015–19 to −2.9% since 2025. Third, the features the trees leaned on most are the ones the screen already flagged — REER, short-term reversal, realised volatility, advance-decline, inflation — so there is no hidden factor the univariate screen missed.

The soft top-k numbers track the hard basket at the sharp setting (ridge 5.2% versus 5.3%) and dilute at the soft setting (3.0% with about 23 effective holdings). The ridge penalty chosen inside the training months scattered across the whole grid from 1 to 1,000, and the inner-CV curve is flat, so the result does not hinge on that choice.

What surprised me: I expected the trees to at least match the linear model on this much tabular data, and they lost to a single factor. The honest reading is that the cross-sectional signal in these 256 inputs is small, mostly linear, and mostly already captured by a handful of known factors.

## stage5

The net beats the broad linear model, by about a point a year, on the same months. As five-seed ensembles on the thirty random splits, the three nets make 6.3% (predict-then-select), 6.4% (soft top-8 objective) and 6.5% a year (warm-start then soft) over the equal-weight average, against ridge's 5.3%. Split by split the net wins 20 to 22 of 30 and the paired difference has a t-statistic between 2.1 and 2.3. Pooled over every evaluation month, the nets' edge has a t-statistic above 6 and a hit rate of 0.64. On the contiguous blocks they hold up at least as well as ridge: the predict-then-select net makes 4.7% a year against ridge's 3.2%, though five blocks is a thin basis and the spread is wide.

The caveat that should sit next to the headline: **all of the edge over ridge is in the first decade.** Paired month by month, the net beats ridge by about 4% a year in 2000–2009, with a t-statistic between 2.5 and 3.0, and by nothing at all in 2010–2019 and 2020–2026, where the differences are within half a point either way with t-statistics near zero. The same shows in the blocks: the net wins the two blocks covering 2000–2010 clearly and ties the three later ones. Everyone's baskets are bigger in the 2000s because the cross-section was wider then — 5.3% monthly dispersion against 3.8% in the 2010s — but that scales ridge too, so the net found something extra in those years specifically. Whether that is a genuinely nonlinear regime the linear model missed, or the net exploiting the thinner feature coverage of the early rows, is not settled here; the honest reading for now is that since 2010 a well-regularised linear model and a small net are the same thing on this objective.

The three objectives tie. Training on the top-8 basket directly, through the soft selection with an exact gradient, does not beat predicting returns and picking the eight best — on the basket. Where it does show is in the ordering below the cut: the soft objective's long-minus-short spread is 12.9% a year against 11.5% for the MSE net, and its soft-k basket is better (6.2% versus 5.6%). The warm-start variant is nominally best but inside the noise. So the "predict, then select" baseline Astra argued for is vindicated on this data, and the direct objective is a mild improvement in the shape of the ranking rather than in the money.

Two mechanics worth knowing. First, ensembling is doing a third of the work: a single seed makes 4.7 to 5.5% a year, the five-seed average 6.3 to 6.5%, and seeds within a split disagree by about 2% a year. The ensemble is the model; a single trained net is not. Second, the nets memorise heavily — 30% a year on training months for the MSE net, 40 to 43% for the soft objectives, against ridge's 16% — and early stopping fires after a median of about 30 epochs. That gap is the obvious lever for the next stage.

The controls behaved. The shuffled-label net made 0.7% a year on evaluation months, zero within the ±2.9% single-split noise and statistically zero pooled. The capacity check fitted 24 months perfectly — precision 1.00, rank correlation 1.00, basket return equal to perfect foresight — so when there is something to fit, the machinery fits it.

What surprised me: that the direct objective did not win, and that the net's advantage is so cleanly confined to one decade. I expected a small uniform edge or none; a large early edge and then a tie is a more specific finding and the first thing the next stage should try to explain.

## ablation_noreer

REER is not load-bearing. With all four REER columns removed — the T2 and BIS versions in both normalisations — the broad linear model makes 5.2% a year on evaluation months against 5.3% with them, a paired difference of −0.04 with a t-statistic of −0.1; it was worse on 17 of 30 splits and better on 13, which is a coin toss. Its era profile is unchanged too (7.7% in the 2000s without REER against 6.8% with). The ridge result was never REER wearing 256 hats; the information REER carries is spread across the nominal-currency, inflation and rate factors that remain, and a linear combination finds it there just as easily.

The trees are the model that cared. LightGBM regression falls from 3.8% to 1.9% a year, worse on 20 of 30 splits with a t-statistic of −3.6, and `BIS_REER_CS` had been its single most-used feature. Without it the trees lean on realised volatility, short-term reversal and advance-decline instead, and transfer less.

The nets lose a little and keep their shape. The three objectives make 5.8%, 5.9% and 6.1% a year without REER against 6.3 to 6.5% with it — about half a point, worse on 17 to 20 splits of 30, t-statistics between −1.3 and −1.5, so not something to lean on in either direction. On the contiguous blocks the predict-then-select net is unchanged or slightly better (5.2% against 4.7%). The 2000–2009 edge is still there without REER (10.1% against 10.6% a year), so whatever the net found in that decade is not REER either. What does change is the net's margin over the broad linear model: it narrows from about 1.1–1.3 points to 0.5–0.85 points, with t-statistics between 1.0 and 1.5 and wins on 18 to 21 of 30 splits. The shuffled-label control sits at 0.5%, zero within noise.

What to take from it: the broad model's number is robust to dropping its single strongest input, which is the property you wanted. The net's advantage over the broad model is real but small and partly rode on REER; a half-to-one-point edge that needs a 30-split paired test to see is the honest size of it before the hill-climb.

## stage7

The sweep found nothing that beats the base configuration, and it found the opposite of what I expected. Every attempt to regularise the network harder makes it worse on the evaluation months: dropout 0.30 costs 1.2 points a year (t = −3.4, wins only 10 of 30 splits), dropout 0.50 costs 2.0 (t = −4.3), the two "heavy" combinations cost 1.3 and 1.4 (t ≈ −3), and a slower learning rate costs 2.0 (t = −4.9, wins 6 of 30). Those nets also fit their training months far less — 16 to 24% a year against the base's 30% — so the memorisation that looked like the obvious lever was not what was hurting. Early stopping after thirty-odd epochs and the five-seed average were already doing the regularising; constraining the network on top of that just removed signal.

Weight decay is irrelevant: 0.1 and 1.0 land within a tenth of a point of the base. Width matters a little and in the direction of bigger: halving to 32/16 costs half a point (t = −1.1), doubling to 128/64 changes nothing, and quadrupling to 256/128 adds a third of a point on random splits (6.7% against 6.3%, t = 1.1, wins 14 of 30 — a tie) but is the best config on the contiguous blocks by a wide margin, 6.6% against the base's 4.7%. Five blocks is a thin basis, but it is the one result in the sweep that points somewhere, and it is consistent with the regularisation finding: this problem wants more capacity, not less. Against ridge, the 256/128 net is +1.4 points with a paired t of 2.65, the strongest margin any model has shown.

The seed curve is the clean result. A single net averages 4.6% a year; two averaged make 5.3%, three 5.5%, five 6.3%, eight 6.7%, ten 6.8%, and fifteen or twenty stay at 6.6 to 6.7%. Averaging saturates around eight to ten nets and adds a little over two points over a single one — more than any architectural choice. On the blocks the curve is noisier but rises the same way (4.7% at five seeds, 5.3% at twenty).

The base configuration rerun inside the sweep reproduced the stage-5 numbers to four decimal places on every split, so the training is deterministic and the comparisons are exact.

What surprised me: I wrote in the last section that the train–eval gap was "the obvious lever", and it was the wrong lever. The honest picture is that a small, barely-regularised net, stopped early and averaged over ten seeds, is close to the ceiling for these inputs — about 6.5 to 6.8% a year on random splits — and the remaining headroom is in what goes in (global context, cross-country structure), not in how hard the net is squeezed.

## ablation_base256

The hill-climb's recommendation held up when applied: the wider net with ten seeds makes 7.1% a year on evaluation months on the predict-then-select objective, against 6.3% for the stage-5 net, a paired gain of 0.75 points with a t-statistic of 2.6, better on 21 of 30 splits. The soft and warm-start objectives gain less (0.5 and 0.3 points, inside the noise) and the three objectives still tie with one another. On the contiguous blocks the predict-then-select net rises from 4.7% to 6.1% and the warm-start net from 4.3% to 6.6%. Against the broad linear model on the same splits the new base is 1.8 points a year ahead with a t-statistic of 3.2, better on 23 of 30 — the clearest margin any model has shown. Its training-month fit also rose, from 30% to 35% a year, which after the hill-climb is no longer something to worry about. This run is on the v1 panel, with REER still in; the cleaned v3 rerun is the number to carry forward.

## ablation_nogdelt

The net does not need the news block. With all 92 GDELT factors removed, the predict-then-select net makes 6.6% a year against 7.1%, a half-point drop with a t-statistic of −1.4 that the other two objectives do not share (they are up 0.2); ridge is unchanged at 5.4%. On the blocks the predict-then-select net falls from 6.1% to 4.0%, which with five blocks is suggestive rather than a finding. The point of this ablation was the decade question: GDELT only exists from 2015, so it cannot be the source of the 2000–2009 edge directly, but its absence before 2015 shapes the coverage pattern a net could read. Without GDELT the early-decade margin over ridge is still 3.3 points a year with a t-statistic of 2.3. Not GDELT.

## ablation_nopresence

Removing the fifteen per-source presence columns — the net's explicit view of which blocks of data exist in a given row — changes nothing that survives a t-test: the three objectives move by −0.4, +0.2 and +0.3 points, ridge by 0.02. The 2000–2009 margin over ridge is 3.5 points a year with a t-statistic of 2.7, the same as with them. So the early-decade edge is not the net reading the coverage structure of the early rows either. Between this and the GDELT run, what the net found in 2000–2009 is in the factor values of that era. Whether it is economics or a data-vintage artifact of the kind the REER audit found is exactly what the cleaned v3 rerun and the walk-forward are for.

## next

The hill-climb moved the base: 256/128 hidden, dropout 0.15, weight decay 0.01, ten seeds — expected around 6.8% a year on random splits and five to six on blocks, one to one-and-a-half points over ridge. Regularisation is settled (leave it alone) and ensembling is settled (ten seeds). What is left is the inputs and the architecture, in this order:

- **Explain the decade.** Two tagged ablations on the new base: without the GDELT block (it only exists from 2015) and without the per-source presence columns. If the 2000–2009 edge survives both, it is economics; if it goes with the presence columns, the net was reading the coverage structure.
- **Global context.** A v2 panel adding the series that are identical across countries (the global geopolitical-risk indices, FRED spreads, commodity z-scores), which a net can only use through interactions. Expected to inflate random-month scores, so read it against the block diagnostic.
- **Cross-country attention.** The first genuinely different architecture: one country's score can depend on what the other 33 look like that month. Bigger model, GPU-sized, judged on the same splits.
- **Walk-forward.** Once the design settles, the test this report has deliberately not run — train on the past, score the next year, roll — which is what would say whether any of this is a forecast rather than a fit.
